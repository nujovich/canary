Comfort Food

What I miss
when I'm 10,000 km away

🧉

Mate

Not a drink. A ritual. The gourd passes hand to hand. You don't say gracias until you're done — and you're never done after just one. It's the taste of 6 AM before school, of roadside stops in the Pampas, of every conversation that mattered.

🥟

Empanadas

The repulgue — that folded edge — isn't decoration. It's a code. Each shape tells you what's inside: beef, chicken, ham & cheese, humita. My grandmother could close a dozen in two minutes flat. I still can't get the fold right.

🥩

Asado

Sunday. The smell of quebracho wood. My dad standing by the parrilla for three hours, beer in hand, talking about fútbol. We didn't call it "barbecue" — we called it church. Everyone shows up. Nobody leaves early.

🍰

Alfajores

Two biscuits, dulce de leche, coconut shavings. The corner kiosko sold them for 50 centavos when I was a kid. Now I pay €4 for a Havanna box in Barcelona and pretend I'm not homesick while I eat all six in one sitting.

🍝

Ñoquis del 29

Every 29th of the month: gnocchi. Money under the plate for good luck. It's an Argentine-Italian thing nobody quite remembers the origin of, but everybody observes. Even here, even now, I still put a €2 coin under my bowl.

🍦

Helado

Argentines eat more ice cream per capita than almost anyone. Not just in summer — year round. The heladerías are open until 2 AM. A kilo of dulce de leche granizado on a Tuesday night is a perfectly normal decision.

The Table

No hate survives
a sobremesa

Immigrants sit at the table they built. We bring dishes that crossed oceans, recipes carried in memory, the accent we refused to lose.

Argentina had a rough year. The world watched, judged, labeled. But a country isn't a headline — it's the people who stay behind, the ones who left, and the meals that still taste like home no matter which continent you're on.

A sobremesa is proof. You stay at the table not for the food, but for the people. For the stories. For the argument that turns into laughter. For the cousin who brings up politics and the grandmother who changes the subject. For the hour that becomes three.

You can't hate someone whose milanesa you just ate. At the very least, you owe them a conversation.

Pull up a chair.

Kitchen

Recipes from
three generations

Abuela's Empanadas Tucumanas +

My grandmother was from Tucumán, where empanadas are fried, not baked. The filling is cut by knife — never ground. The secret is the lemon juice in the meat.

  1. Cut 500g beef into tiny cubes (not ground). Marinate with lemon juice, cumin, paprika, salt.
  2. Sauté onion and green pepper in beef fat until translucent. Add meat, cook 5 min.
  3. Add chopped hard-boiled egg and green olives. Let cool completely.
  4. Fill empanada discs, seal with repulgue (folded edge). Fry in hot oil until golden.
  5. Serve with chimichurri and a glass of Torrontés. Wait for the argument about which province makes the best empanadas.
Mamá's Milanesa Napolitana +

A German dish made Italian in Argentina: breaded beef cutlet topped with ham, tomato sauce, and melted cheese. My mom made it every Wednesday for 20 years.

  1. Pound beef cutlets thin. Season with salt, pepper, garlic.
  2. Dip in beaten egg with parsley, then breadcrumbs.
  3. Fry in plenty of oil until golden on both sides.
  4. Top with ham slice, tomato sauce, and mozzarella. Broil until bubbly.
  5. Serve with French fries (the real ones, cut thick). Wednesday is now the best day of the week.
Mine: Mate as ritual +

Mate isn't cooked — it's prepared. And the preparation is the ritual. This is how I taught my Spanish friends.

  1. Fill a mate (gourd) 3/4 with yerba mate. Tilt it so the yerba forms a slope.
  2. Pour warm water (not boiling — never boiling) into the empty side. Let the yerba absorb.
  3. Insert the bombilla (metal straw) into the wet side. Don't move it again.
  4. Pour hot water (70-80°C, not boiling) into the empty space. Drink all the water in one go — slurping is allowed, encouraged even.
  5. Pass to the next person. The cebador (server) refills and passes to each person in turn. Don't say gracias until you're done for real — once you do, you won't be served again.
